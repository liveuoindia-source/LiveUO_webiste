The ten numbers a pharmacy owner should see every week

Not a dashboard of everything. The short list of measures that actually change a decision, why each one earns its place, and what to do when it moves.

Most pharmacy dashboards show too much. Forty tiles, thirty of which nobody acts on, and the three that matter buried among them. The test for any number is simple: if this moves, what will I do differently? If there is no answer, it does not belong on a weekly review.

Here are ten that pass the test, and what each one is for.

1. Sales value, and bill count, versus the same week last year

Two numbers, not one. Sales alone cannot distinguish more customers from the same customers spending more, and those call for different responses.

  • Bills up, average value flat → footfall is growing
  • Bills flat, average value up → basket is growing, or prices moved
  • Bills down, sales flat → you are losing customers and masking it with larger baskets

Compare against the same week last year, not last week. Pharmacy demand is seasonal and week-on-week comparison mostly measures the calendar.

2. Gross margin value, not just percentage

Rupees, and the percentage alongside it. Percentage alone hides mix: a shift toward a lower-margin category reduces the percentage without anything being wrong. Value tells you what actually funds the business.

When margin value falls while sales hold, the cause is in one of the next three numbers.

3. Discount as a percentage of gross sales, by user

The most commonly leaked margin in an independent pharmacy, and the easiest to recover once visible.

Watch the trend rather than the level — discount creep is gradual and invisible month to month. A per-user breakdown turns it from an abstract problem into a specific conversation. The margin analysis covers how to read it properly.

4. Stock value within 120 days of expiry

The most under-watched number in retail pharmacy, and the most valuable leading indicator you have.

Write-off value tells you about purchasing decisions made a year ago. Near-expiry value tells you about a loss that has not happened yet and can still be prevented — by supplier return, branch transfer, or promotion. Watch this one and the write-off number takes care of itself. The mechanisms are here.

5. Stockouts on your top 200 lines

Lost sales are invisible in every sales report, which is precisely why they persist.

Track how many of your top 200 items were unavailable at any point in the week. Each one is a customer who either bought a substitute, went elsewhere, or came back later — and one of those three outcomes is a customer you may not get back.

This is the number that justifies improving purchase suggestions.

6. Days of stock cover, overall and for the slowest quartile

Total stock value divided by average daily cost of sales. Then the same for your slowest-moving quartile specifically.

The overall figure tells you about working capital. The slow-quartile figure tells you where it is trapped — and that is usually where next year's expiry write-off is currently sitting on a shelf looking harmless.

7. Outstanding receivables, aged

Total, plus the ageing distribution. Two pharmacies with identical outstanding are in completely different positions if one is current and the other is ninety days past due.

Watch the movement between buckets, week to week. Money moving from 30–60 into 60–90 is a collection problem developing, and it is much cheaper to address there than later. The credit management practices set out what to do.

8. Repeat customer share

The proportion of sales to customers who bought from you in the previous 90 days.

This is the health of your chronic-patient base, which is the most defensible revenue a pharmacy has. A falling repeat share means you are churning customers — and it falls before total sales do, which makes it worth more than the sales number itself.

9. Compliance exceptions

A short list, reviewed weekly:

  • Scheduled-drug sales missing prescriber or patient details
  • Price overrides above authorised limits
  • Bills cancelled after completion
  • Stock adjustments outside the count cycle
  • Controlled-substance count discrepancies

Each of these should be near zero. The value is not in the number; it is in noticing when it stops being zero. A cluster around one user or one shift is a training issue found early rather than an inspection finding found late.

10. Cash and settlement reconciliation

Cash counted versus cash expected. Card and UPI settlement received versus recorded.

Daily is better, weekly is the minimum. Small persistent differences compound and become impossible to investigate once they are three months old.

What deliberately is not on the list

  • Footfall counters — interesting, rarely actionable
  • Category-level detail — a monthly review, not weekly
  • Item-level sales rankings — a purchasing input, not an owner metric
  • Anything with no decision attached to it

The discipline of keeping the list short is what makes it get read.

Making the review happen

Ten numbers, one page, same day each week, fifteen minutes.

Three practices that make it stick:

Have it delivered. A scheduled report that arrives is read; one that must be pulled is not.

Annotate. One line on anything that moved materially, and what you decided. Six months of those lines is the most useful management record a small business can have.

Review per branch and consolidated. Averages across branches hide the branch with the problem. For multi-branch operations, the comparison between outlets is often more informative than the total.

The monthly and quarterly layer

Weekly is for operations. Slower cycles carry the structural questions:

CadenceReview
MonthlyMargin by category with share movement; write-off value; returns by reason; new credit extended
QuarterlyPurchase rate variance on top 200; bottom-50 margin items; dead stock; supplier terms and fill rates
Half-yearlySupplier negotiation armed with rate variance, fill rate and return-rate data
