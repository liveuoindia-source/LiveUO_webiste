Build vs buy: when custom software is worth it, and when it isn't

A practical way to decide between off-the-shelf software and a custom build, based on how unique your process is, total cost over five years, and the risk of each.

  2. When buying is the right answer
  3. When building makes sense
  4. Compare five-year cost, not the first invoice
  5. The hybrid option

Most businesses reach the build-or-buy question the same way: the spreadsheets have grown too large, the off-the-shelf tool doesn't quite fit, and someone suggests "we should just build our own".

Sometimes that is exactly right. Often it isn't. The decision deserves more than a gut feeling, because both wrong answers are expensive — one in money, the other in years of working around software that fights your process.

Start with the process, not the software

Ask one question first: is this process what makes our business different?

  • Accounting, payroll, email and standard billing are the same in most businesses. There is little advantage in doing them differently, and mature products already exist. Buy.
  • The way you quote complex jobs, schedule specialised field teams, or manage a supply chain unique to your trade may be exactly why customers choose you. Forcing it into generic software can erode that advantage. Consider building.

A useful test: if a competitor bought the same packaged software, would they gain your edge? If yes, the process is generic. If no, it may be worth owning.

When buying is the right answer

Buy when:

  • a well-established product covers 80% or more of what you need without heavy customisation
  • the process follows industry or regulatory standards that the vendor keeps up to date
  • you need it working within weeks, not months
  • you don't want to own maintenance, hosting and security
  • the vendor has a track record and other customers like you

The remaining 20% is usually better solved by adjusting your process than by fighting the software.

When building makes sense

Build, or extend, when:

  • packaged options cover only part of the process and the gaps are core work, not edge cases
  • you are paying for several tools and re-entering data between them
  • per-user licence costs grow faster than the business
  • staff spend hours each day on workarounds — exports, copy-paste, side spreadsheets
  • you need integrations the vendors won't provide
  • the software itself becomes part of what you sell or how you serve customers

Compare five-year cost, not the first invoice

The first-year price misleads in both directions. Lay out everything over five years:

CostBuyBuild
UpfrontSetup, configuration, data migrationDiscovery, design, development
RecurringLicences per user or branchHosting, maintenance, support
ChangePaid customisation, if possible at allNew features at your pace
HiddenWorkarounds, double entry, extra toolsYour team's time in planning and testing
ExitData export limits, switching costCode ownership, handover

A packaged product at a modest monthly fee per user can overtake a custom build within a few years if your headcount grows. A custom build that nobody maintains becomes a liability. Model both honestly.

The hybrid option

The best answer is often not either-or. Buy the commodity layers — accounting, email, payment gateways — and build a focused application for the part that's yours, connected to the rest through APIs.

This keeps the custom part small, which keeps it cheaper to build, faster to deliver and easier to maintain.

Reduce the risk of building

If you decide to build, protect the decision:

  1. Write down the scope. Screens, rules, reports and integrations, agreed before development starts.
  2. Start with the smallest useful version. Release the core workflow first; add the rest once people are using it.
  3. Involve the people who will use it. Not just managers — the staff doing the work daily know where the real exceptions are.
  4. Agree on ownership. Who owns the code, where it is hosted, and how you get it if the relationship ends.
  5. Budget for after launch. Plan for support, fixes and improvements, not just the build.

A quick decision checklist

  • Is this process a competitive advantage? If no, lean buy.
  • Does an existing product cover at least 80% without heavy customisation? If yes, lean buy.
  • Do workarounds cost hours every day? If yes, lean build.
  • Do you need integrations vendors won't provide? If yes, lean build or hybrid.
  • Can you commit people to define and test the software? If no, building will struggle.

There is no universally right answer. There is only the answer that fits the process you run, the cost over the years you'll run it, and the risk you can carry.
