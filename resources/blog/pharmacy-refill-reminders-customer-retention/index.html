Refill reminders and repeat customers: retention for a retail pharmacy

Chronic patients are the most predictable revenue a pharmacy has, and the easiest to lose. How refill timing works, what to send, and where consent and privacy limits sit.

The most predictable revenue in a retail pharmacy comes from chronic patients — hypertension, diabetes, thyroid, cardiac — who need the same medication every month for years. They are also the easiest customers to lose, because switching is frictionless: a pharmacy nearer to the clinic, a slightly faster counter, or simply being out of stock once at the wrong moment.

Refill reminders are the cheapest retention mechanism available, and most pharmacies either do not run them or run them badly. Here is how to run them well.

The calculation behind the timing

A reminder sent at the wrong time is worse than none — too early and it is nagging, too late and the customer has already bought elsewhere.

The inputs:

  • Quantity dispensed
  • Dosage — units per day
  • Days of supply = quantity ÷ daily dose
  • Lead — send two to four days before supply runs out

Quantity alone is insufficient and is the reason simplistic reminder systems get ignored. Thirty tablets is thirty days at one daily and ten days at three times daily. Without dosage, your reminder is a guess with a two-in-three chance of being wrong.

That means dosage has to be captured at dispensing. It is one extra field, and it is what separates a reminder system that works from one that annoys people.

What to actually send

Three principles.

Be useful, not promotional. "Your repeat is due around the 14th — shall we keep it ready?" is a service. "GREAT OFFERS THIS WEEK" is a broadcast, and it trains people to ignore you.

Make the response one step. A reply that reserves the stock. If responding requires a phone call during business hours, most people will simply not.

Be discreet. This is the part most often got wrong.

A message reading "Your Metformin refill is due" tells anyone who glances at that phone that the person has diabetes. Phones are shared, screens are visible, and lock-screen previews are on by default. Name the medicine only where the customer has specifically asked you to.

A neutral formulation — "your monthly repeat is due on the 14th" — carries the same operational information with none of the disclosure.

Frequency, and knowing when to stop

  • One reminder per refill cycle. A second one, at most, only if the first went unanswered and the medication is one where a gap matters clinically.
  • Stop after two unanswered cycles. They have moved, or they have another supplier. Continuing costs goodwill.
  • Never contact more often than the supply cycle. Monthly supply means at most monthly contact.
  • Honour opt-out immediately and permanently.

Consent, and where the line sits

Contact details in a pharmacy are personal data, and a purchase history that implies a medical condition is sensitive by nature. Under the DPDP Act, 2023 you need a lawful basis and a purpose for the processing.

Practically:

  1. Ask at the counter — "would you like us to remind you when your repeat is due?"
  2. Record the answer against the customer record, with the date
  3. Include an opt-out in every message
  4. Act on opt-out immediately, and keep the record that they opted out
  5. Do not repurpose the list. Consent to refill reminders is not consent to promotional messaging

The related data protection obligations cover the wider picture, but the counter-level rule is simple: ask, record, respect.

Beyond reminders: what retains chronic patients

Reminders are the mechanism. The relationship is what actually retains.

Reserve stock for known repeats. If you know forty patients need a particular formulation this month, that is not a forecast, it is a commitment. Running out for a regular is the single most effective way to lose one.

Remember the preference. Same brand, same pack size, same substitution acceptance. Having it noted means not asking every month.

Make collection quick. A repeat that is pre-packed and handed over in ninety seconds beats a competitor's price difference for most people.

Offer delivery where it matters. Elderly and mobility-limited patients are the most loyal customers you will ever have if collection is the friction point.

Measuring whether it works

Four numbers, monthly:

MetricWhat it tells you
Refill adherence rateProportion of expected repeats that actually returned
Reminder response rateWhether your message and timing are right
Lapsed chronic patientsCustomers with no repeat for two cycles — your churn
Repeat revenue shareHow much of turnover is predictable

The third is the one to act on. A lapsed-patient list is a short, specific, high-value call list — and a phone call asking whether everything is alright recovers a meaningful share of them.

What not to do

  • Do not buy a contact list. Beyond the legal exposure, it does not work.
  • Do not send bulk promotional messages to a health-related list. The reputational risk is asymmetric.
  • Do not automate the clinical judgement. A reminder is an administrative prompt, not advice. Anything approaching a clinical suggestion belongs with the pharmacist, in person.
  • Do not let the reminder replace stock discipline. Reminding someone their repeat is due and then not having it is worse than silence.
