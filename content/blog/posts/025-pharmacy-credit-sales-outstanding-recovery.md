Almost every pharmacy extends credit, and almost none of them manage it. It accumulates through ordinary kindness — a regular customer short this week, a doctor's clinic taking supplies, a corporate account someone agreed to years ago — until a meaningful share of the pharmacy's working capital is sitting in other people's cash flow.

Credit is not the problem. Undocumented, unlimited, unaged credit is.

## Decide who gets credit, and write it down

Three categories, with different rules:

**Institutional accounts** — clinics, nursing homes, corporate tie-ups. Genuine B2B business, usually with agreed terms and a purchase order or an approved list. Should be formal, with a written agreement, a limit and terms.

**Professional accounts** — local doctors' clinics buying supplies. Relationship-driven, and the relationship is often worth more than the receivable. Still needs a limit.

**Neighbourhood credit** — regular customers, small amounts, informal. Individually trivial, collectively significant, and the hardest to control because each instance feels too small to bother with.

The third category is where most uncontrolled exposure sits. It needs the lightest process, but it needs one.

## Set limits, and let the system enforce them

A credit limit that only exists in someone's head is not a limit. Practically:

- **Limit per customer**, based on normal monthly purchase and payment history
- **Enforced at billing** — the system warns or blocks when a sale would exceed it
- **Override permitted but logged**, with who and why
- **Reviewed** as history accumulates — good payers earn more room, slow payers less

Start conservatively for new accounts. Raising a limit is a pleasant conversation; reducing one is not.

> The value of enforcement is not that it stops sales. It is that it converts an invisible drift into a visible decision made by a named person at a specific moment.

## Age the receivable, always

A single outstanding total tells you almost nothing. Ageing tells you what to do.

| Bucket | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0–30 days | Normal | None |
| 31–60 | Slipping | Reminder with statement |
| 61–90 | A problem | Direct call, agree a date |
| 91–180 | Serious | Stop further credit; agree a plan |
| 180+ | Likely impaired | Recovery decision; provision it |

Two accounts owing the same amount, one entirely current and one entirely over ninety days, are completely different situations. A blended total hides that.

## Collect early, specifically, and politely

The determining factor in recovery is **how early you make contact**, not how firmly.

**Day 7 after due:** a friendly reminder with a statement attached. Most of what gets paid, gets paid here. It costs nothing and damages nothing — people genuinely forget.

**Day 30:** a phone call. Not a demand — a check that the statement is correct and agreement on when payment will come.

**Day 60:** a conversation about the account, including whether further credit continues.

**Day 90+:** a payment plan, or a decision about recovery.

Two details that materially change outcomes:

**Send a statement, not a total.** A list of individual invoices with dates and amounts is easy to verify and hard to dispute vaguely. "You owe ₹48,000" invites "are you sure?". A list invites payment.

**Make paying easy.** A UPI QR on the statement removes the friction of the payer having to do anything except approve. A surprising share of ageing receivables are simply an unactioned intention.

## What to watch monthly

- **Total outstanding**, and as a percentage of monthly sales
- **Ageing distribution**, and how it moved
- **Days sales outstanding** — the trend matters more than the level
- **New credit extended** in the month
- **Accounts over limit**, and who authorised the overrides
- **Write-offs** taken

The override list is the diagnostic one. If overrides are frequent, either your limits are set too low for the business you actually do, or the enforcement is not being respected — and those need opposite responses.

## The relationship problem, addressed honestly

The reason pharmacies do not collect is that the customer is a neighbour, or a doctor who refers patients, and asking feels like it risks something.

Three things that help:

**Make it systematic, not personal.** A statement sent to every account on the same day of the month is administration. A call to one person is a confrontation. Systematic collection is easier to do and easier to receive.

**Separate the roles.** The person who sends statements should ideally not be the person behind the counter with whom the customer has a relationship.

**Recognise the actual risk.** A doctor's clinic worth substantial monthly business is worth protecting. A customer who has owed ₹4,000 for eight months and is quietly avoiding the shop has already been lost — declining further credit does not lose them again.

## Interest, discounts, and other levers

**Early payment discount** — a small percentage for payment within a short window. Often more effective than any penalty, because it is a positive interaction, and it is cheap relative to the cost of the capital tied up.

**Late payment interest** — legitimate for institutional accounts with written terms, rarely worth pursuing on small neighbourhood balances where the goodwill cost exceeds the recovery.

**Deposit or advance** — reasonable for large institutional accounts, particularly new ones with no payment history.
