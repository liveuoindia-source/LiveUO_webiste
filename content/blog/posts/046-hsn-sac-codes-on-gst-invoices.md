Every item on a GST invoice belongs to a classification. For goods, that's an HSN code; for services, a SAC code. The code decides the tax rate, appears in your returns, and for e-invoicing businesses is validated by the invoice registration portal.

Many small businesses treat the code as a formality, copied from a supplier's invoice or guessed once and never checked. That works until a return mismatch, an e-invoice rejection or a notice arrives.

## HSN and SAC in brief

- **HSN** — Harmonised System of Nomenclature — is an international system for classifying goods, used by customs worldwide and adopted for GST. Codes run from 2 digits (chapter) to 8 digits (tariff item).
- **SAC** — Services Accounting Code — classifies services under GST. SAC codes typically start with 99.

The more digits, the more specific the classification. Chapter 30, for example, covers pharmaceutical products; longer codes within it distinguish types of products.

## How many digits do you need?

The number of HSN digits required on invoices depends on your **aggregate turnover in the previous financial year**, and sometimes on whether the supply is to a registered business (B2B) or a consumer (B2C). Larger businesses must show more digits.

The thresholds have been revised before. Check the current notification on the GST portal or with your tax adviser, and recheck each year — growing past a threshold changes your requirement.

GSTR-1 also requires an HSN-wise summary of supplies, with digits as required for your turnover. Getting codes right on invoices makes that summary accurate automatically.

## Finding the right code

1. **Start with what the product is**, not what a supplier wrote. Suppliers make mistakes too.
2. **Search the GST portal's HSN/SAC lookup** by description.
3. **Read the chapter notes.** Classification often depends on what the product is made of or its main use, and the notes explain the boundaries.
4. **Check the rate schedule** to confirm the tax rate that applies to that code.
5. **Ask your tax adviser** for anything ambiguous — mixed products, kits, or items that could fall under two headings.

Record your reasoning for unusual items. If the classification is ever questioned, a documented decision is much easier to defend.

## Common mistakes

- **Using one code for everything** in a category, regardless of product differences.
- **Copying supplier codes** without checking.
- **Too few digits** after the business grows past a threshold.
- **Wrong code for services**, especially businesses that sell both goods and services, such as installation or annual maintenance.
- **Updating the rate but not the code**, or the reverse, after a GST rate change.

## Why it matters

| Problem | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Wrong code | Possibly the wrong tax rate on every sale |
| Missing digits | Non-compliant invoices and returns |
| Inconsistent codes | HSN summary in GSTR-1 doesn't match reality |
| Invalid code on e-invoice | Invoice rejected by the portal |
| Customer's credit affected | Disputes with business customers |

## Let the item master do the work

The best place to get HSN right is once, in the item master — not at the counter.

Good billing software should:

- store the HSN or SAC code and tax rate on every item
- prevent saving an item without a code
- apply the code and rate to invoices automatically
- validate the number of digits against your requirement
- produce the HSN-wise summary for GSTR-1
- make bulk updates easy when rates change

Counter staff should never have to choose a code during a sale.

## A quick clean-up plan

1. Export your item list with codes and rates.
2. Sort by code and look for items with missing codes, short codes, or codes that look wrong for the product.
3. Check high-volume items first — they carry the most risk.
4. Correct the master and keep a note of changes.
5. Review new items as they're added.

Getting HSN and SAC codes right is mostly a one-time effort with a small ongoing check. It removes a whole category of return mismatches and e-invoice rejections, and it means your tax rates rest on something more solid than a guess.
