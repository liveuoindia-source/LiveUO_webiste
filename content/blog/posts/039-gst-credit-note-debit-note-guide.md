Invoices rarely stay exactly as issued. Goods come back, prices are corrected, discounts are agreed after the sale, and sometimes the original bill was simply wrong. Under GST, you don't edit or delete an issued tax invoice to fix these. You issue a credit note or a debit note.

Getting these right keeps your GST returns accurate and saves long reconciliation arguments with customers.

## Credit note: reducing what was billed

A credit note is issued by the supplier when the value or tax on an invoice should be **lower** than originally charged. Common reasons:

- the customer returns goods
- the goods or service supplied were deficient
- the invoice overstated the price or quantity
- a discount linked to the original sale is given afterwards, under terms agreed in advance

The credit note reduces your output tax for the period in which you report it, and the customer's input tax credit falls correspondingly.

## Debit note: increasing what was billed

A debit note is issued by the supplier when the value or tax should be **higher** than invoiced:

- the price was undercharged by mistake
- additional charges were agreed after billing
- the quantity supplied was more than invoiced

It increases your output tax, and the customer can claim the additional input tax credit.

You may also hear "debit note" used by a buyer for a purchase return. For GST purposes, the document that changes the tax is the supplier's credit note. A buyer's debit note is a commercial document asking for it.

## What a credit or debit note must contain

At a minimum, include:

- your name, address and GSTIN
- a serial number, unique within the financial year
- date of issue
- the recipient's name, address and GSTIN (for registered recipients)
- the original invoice number and date
- taxable value, tax rate and tax amount being adjusted
- signature or digital signature

If you're under GST e-invoicing, credit and debit notes also need to be registered on the invoice registration portal.

## Time limits matter

Credit notes have a deadline linked to the financial year of the original supply. Adjustments made after it cannot reduce your tax liability, even if you've refunded the customer. Rules change, so confirm the current deadline with your tax adviser and, more importantly, don't let credit notes pile up for year-end.

A practical rule: issue the credit note when the return or adjustment is agreed, not weeks later.

## Discounts: the common trap

Not every discount can go through a credit note. A post-sale discount reduces taxable value only when it was agreed before or at the time of supply and linked to specific invoices. A discretionary discount given afterwards, without such an agreement, is generally a commercial credit note that doesn't reduce GST.

If your business relies on volume rebates or year-end discounts, put the terms in writing at the start of the year and get your tax adviser to confirm how they should be documented.

## How billing software should handle them

Credit and debit notes are where manual billing breaks down most often. Good software should:

1. **Create a credit note from the original invoice**, pulling in items, rates and tax so you only change quantities or amounts.
2. **Prevent over-crediting**, such as returning more than was sold.
3. **Keep a separate numbering series** for credit and debit notes.
4. **Adjust stock automatically** for returned goods, and allow damaged returns to be kept out of saleable stock.
5. **Show them correctly in GST reports**, linked to the original invoice.
6. **Register them on the e-invoice portal** if e-invoicing applies to you.

## Reconcile with your customers

Business customers claim input tax credit based on what you report. If your credit note isn't reported, or is reported in a different period, their numbers won't match yours. Share credit notes promptly and reconcile balances with major customers monthly, so differences are cleared before returns are filed.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Document | Effect on your output tax |
|---|---|---|
| Goods returned | Credit note | Decreases |
| Price overcharged | Credit note | Decreases |
| Agreed post-sale discount | Credit note | Decreases |
| Price undercharged | Debit note | Increases |
| Extra charges agreed later | Debit note | Increases |

Credit and debit notes are routine documents. Handled consistently — linked to invoices, issued on time, and reconciled with customers — they keep your returns accurate. Left to month-end, they become the reason the books don't tie up.
