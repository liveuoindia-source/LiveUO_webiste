The GST composition scheme is designed for small businesses: lower compliance, fewer returns, and tax paid as a flat percentage of turnover. It also changes how you bill — and billing software built for regular GST taxpayers can get it wrong in ways that cause real trouble.

## What the composition scheme is

Under the scheme, an eligible business pays tax at a fixed rate on its turnover instead of charging GST on each sale and claiming input tax credit on purchases. Eligibility depends on turnover limits and the type of business, with restrictions on certain activities. Because limits and conditions are revised from time to time, confirm the current rules with your tax adviser before opting in.

## How billing changes

### Bill of supply, not tax invoice

A composition taxpayer issues a **bill of supply** instead of a tax invoice. The key differences:

- **no GST is shown or collected** from the customer
- the price charged is simply the price — tax is the business's own cost
- the bill must carry the prescribed declaration that the seller is a composition taxable person, not eligible to collect tax

### No input tax credit — for anyone

You can't claim input tax credit on your purchases, and your customers can't claim credit on what they buy from you. For B2C businesses this rarely matters. For businesses selling to GST-registered buyers, it can be a reason those buyers prefer a regular supplier.

### Display at the premises

The words declaring composition status must also be displayed at the place of business, as prescribed.

## What billing software must do

Software set up for regular GST taxpayers will, by default, calculate and print tax on every invoice. For a composition dealer, that's wrong. The software should:

1. **Have a composition mode** that switches the document to a bill of supply.
2. **Suppress tax lines** — no CGST, SGST or IGST shown or collected.
3. **Print the required declaration** on every bill.
4. **Still record HSN codes** where required, and track turnover by rate category for returns.
5. **Calculate composition tax payable** on turnover for the period, for your quarterly payment.
6. **Track purchases** without claiming credit, including any reverse-charge purchases where tax is payable by you.

## Common mistakes

- **Charging GST anyway**, out of habit or because the software defaulted to it. Tax collected that shouldn't have been creates liabilities.
- **Using "tax invoice" as the heading** on bills.
- **Forgetting the declaration** on bills or at the shop.
- **Ignoring turnover limits.** Crossing the limit during the year means moving to regular GST from the date it's crossed, with all invoicing rules changing.
- **Making ineligible supplies**, such as inter-state supplies of goods, which can disqualify the business.

## When to leave the scheme

Composition may stop making sense when:

- turnover approaches the limit
- you start selling to many GST-registered businesses who want input credit
- you want to sell across states or through e-commerce platforms
- your purchases carry a lot of GST that you'd like to claim as credit

Switching to regular GST changes everything about billing: tax invoices, tax collected per line, input tax credit and monthly or quarterly returns. Choose software that can make that switch cleanly — with a clear cut-over date, new numbering series if needed, and correct treatment of stock held on the date of change, which has its own credit rules.

## Quick comparison

| | Composition | Regular GST |
|---|---|---|
| Document | Bill of supply | Tax invoice |
| Tax shown to customer | No | Yes |
| Input tax credit | Not available | Available |
| Customer's credit | Not available | Available (B2B) |
| Returns | Simpler, less frequent | Monthly or quarterly |

The composition scheme can make life genuinely simpler for a small business. The key is billing software that respects its rules — and is ready for the day you outgrow it.
