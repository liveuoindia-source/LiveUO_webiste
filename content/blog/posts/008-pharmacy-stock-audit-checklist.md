Most pharmacy stock audits produce a number, adjust the system to match, and change nothing. That is a stocktake, not an audit. The point of counting is to find out **why** the system and the shelf disagree, because the causes — mis-entered receipts, unrecorded returns, expired stock removed informally, or theft — each need a different fix and each will recur until they are addressed.

This is a procedure that finds causes.

## Before you count: run four reports

Counting cold wastes the count. Spend an hour first.

**1. Negative stock report.** Any line showing negative quantity is a definite process failure — usually a sale entered before the corresponding purchase. These are guaranteed findings; fix them before the count so they do not contaminate it.

**2. Zero-movement report.** Items with stock but no movement in six months. These are your dead-stock and expiry candidates, and they are also where counting errors hide because nobody touches the shelf.

**3. High-value lines.** The top 50 by stock value. If your variance is concentrated anywhere, it is here, and this is where investigation pays.

**4. Adjustment history since the last count.** Who has been adjusting stock, how often, and for what reason. A pattern here often explains the variance before you count a single box.

## Choose the right kind of count

| Type | Scope | Frequency | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full count | Everything, shop closed | Annual | Year-end, statutory position |
| Cycle count | A rotating slice | Weekly | Finding causes early |
| Targeted count | One category or supplier | On suspicion | Investigating a known problem |
| Perpetual spot check | A few lines daily | Daily | High-value or controlled items |

Cycle counting is the highest-value habit here. Counting 40 lines a week means every item is verified two or three times a year without ever closing the shop, and — critically — a variance surfaces within weeks of the event that caused it, while the trail is still findable.

## The count itself

**Freeze movement.** No sales, receipts or transfers during the count window for the section being counted. If the shop must trade, count section by section and quarantine anything transacted mid-count.

**Count by batch, blind.** Count sheets should list product, batch and location but **not the expected quantity**. A pre-filled quantity is an invitation to confirm rather than count, and it destroys the value of the exercise.

**Two people on high-value lines.** One counts, one records. Controlled substances get counted twice by different people.

**Record location.** If an item is found somewhere it should not be, note it — misplacement is one of the most common causes of "missing" stock, and it is fixable by relayout rather than by discipline.

> The single biggest determinant of audit quality is whether the count sheet shows the expected figure. Blind counts find variance. Pre-filled counts find agreement.

## Investigate before you adjust

This is the step that gets skipped. For every line over your variance threshold, work through the sequence:

1. **Recount.** A meaningful share of variances are counting errors. Recount before anything else.
2. **Check for the item elsewhere** — a second shelf, the back store, a fridge, a pending transfer, a customer hold.
3. **Check goods receipt.** Was a recent purchase entered with the wrong quantity, wrong pack size, or against the wrong batch? Pack-size confusion — strips versus tablets — is the most common single cause.
4. **Check returns.** Customer returns taken back without an entry, or supplier returns dispatched without one, both show as variance.
5. **Check for informal disposal.** Damaged or expired stock removed from the shelf and not written off in the system.
6. **Check the sale lines** for the batch. If stock left as sales you cannot account for, that is a different conversation.

Only after that do you post the adjustment — with the **reason recorded on the line**. An adjustment reason field that is always blank is a field that is teaching you nothing.

## What to do with the findings

The output of an audit should be two things, not one:

**The adjusted stock position** — necessary, but the lesser output.

**A short list of process fixes.** For example: pack-size errors concentrated on one supplier's lines means the item master needs its pack representation corrected. Variances concentrated on one shift means goods receipt training. Expired stock found on shelf means near-expiry alerting is not being actioned.

Track the variance value as a percentage of stock value, count over count. If it is not falling, the audit is a ritual.

## Controlled substances are a separate discipline

Schedule X and narcotic stock is not part of the general count. It is counted separately, more often, by two people, against the dedicated register, with any discrepancy escalated immediately rather than adjusted. The record-keeping obligations here are legal rather than commercial, and the tolerance for variance is zero. The [drug-schedule record requirements](/resources/blog/schedule-h-h1-h1x-pharmacy-billing-software/) set out what those registers must contain.
