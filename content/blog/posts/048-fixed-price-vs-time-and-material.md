Once you've decided to build custom software, the next question is how to pay for it. Most development partners offer two models: a fixed price for an agreed scope, or time and material, where you pay for the hours actually worked.

Neither is cheaper by nature. Each puts the risk in a different place, and the right choice depends on how well you know what you want.

## Fixed price

You agree a scope, a price and a timeline. The developer delivers that scope for that price.

**Works well when:**

- requirements are clear and written down
- the project is small to medium in size
- you need a firm budget for approval
- the technology and integrations are well understood

**Risks:**

- **Padding.** The developer prices in uncertainty, so a vague scope means a higher quote.
- **Rigidity.** Ideas that come up during the project become change requests with extra cost.
- **Scope disputes.** "Was that included?" becomes a frequent conversation if the document was thin.
- **Corner-cutting pressure.** If the developer underestimated, quality can suffer as they try to finish within budget.

## Time and material

You pay an agreed rate for the time spent, usually billed monthly or by milestone.

**Works well when:**

- requirements are still evolving, or you're building something new
- you want to adjust priorities as you see working software
- the project is long-running, with ongoing improvements
- you have someone available to make decisions regularly

**Risks:**

- **Open-ended cost.** Without discipline, the budget grows.
- **Effort on your side.** You need to prioritise, review and decide often.
- **Less predictability** for budget approvals.

## A side-by-side view

| | Fixed price | Time and material |
|---|---|---|
| Budget certainty | High, if scope is clear | Lower, controlled by caps |
| Flexibility | Low | High |
| Who carries estimate risk | Developer (priced in) | You |
| Effort needed from you | Mostly at the start | Throughout |
| Best for | Well-defined projects | Evolving or long-term projects |

## The hybrid most projects should consider

Many successful projects combine both:

1. **Discovery on time and material.** A short, paid phase where the developer works with your team to define workflows, rules, screens and integrations. The output is a clear scope and design.
2. **Delivery on fixed price**, in phases. Each phase has a defined scope and price based on the discovery work.
3. **Improvements on time and material**, after launch, as you learn what users need.

This gives you budget certainty where the scope is known, and flexibility where it isn't. It also lets you judge the developer on a small engagement before committing to the full build.

## Protect yourself in either model

Whatever you choose, put these in the contract:

- **Scope or backlog** in writing, with priorities
- **Milestones with working software**, not just documents
- **Acceptance criteria** — how you'll decide a deliverable is done
- **Change process** — how changes are requested, estimated and approved
- **Ownership** of code and deliverables, and handover of source code and credentials
- **Support and warranty period** after launch, and what it covers
- **Payment tied to milestones**, not dates alone

On time and material, add a monthly budget cap and regular time reports, and ask for a demo at least every two weeks.

## Which should you choose?

- **Clear scope, fixed budget:** fixed price.
- **Unclear scope:** start with paid discovery, then decide.
- **Ongoing product with changing priorities:** time and material with a cap.
- **Not sure:** hybrid.

The contract model won't rescue a poorly defined project, but the right model makes a well-defined one predictable, and gives an evolving one room to improve without constant renegotiation.
