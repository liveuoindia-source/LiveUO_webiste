Getting a shopper to add something to their cart is hard. Losing them in the last two minutes is painful, and it happens to most of them. Checkout is where small frictions add up: one unexpected charge, one extra form field, one failed payment, and the order is gone.

The good news is that checkout problems are usually fixable, and fixes there improve revenue from traffic you already have.

## First, measure where people leave

Track each step as an analytics event:

1. Viewed cart
2. Started checkout
3. Entered address
4. Chose delivery
5. Reached payment
6. Order confirmed

The step with the biggest drop is where to start. A store that loses most people at address entry has a different problem from one that loses them at payment.

## Common reasons, and fixes

### Surprise costs

Shipping fees, COD charges or taxes that appear only at the last step are among the most common reasons for abandonment.

**Fix:** show delivery charges and any COD fee early — on the product page or cart. If you offer free shipping above a value, show how far the cart is from it.

### Forced account creation

Many first-time buyers won't create an account with a store they've just found.

**Fix:** allow guest checkout with phone number and email. Offer to save details after the order is placed, when the customer already trusts you.

### Too many fields

Every field is a chance to give up, especially on a phone keyboard.

**Fix:** ask only what's needed to deliver. Auto-fill city and state from the PIN code. Use the right keyboard for each field — numeric for phone and PIN. Remember the address for returning customers.

### Slow pages

Checkout pages loaded with scripts, trackers and large images are slow on mobile networks.

**Fix:** keep checkout pages light. Remove anything that isn't needed to complete the order, and test on a mid-range phone on a mobile connection — not just office Wi-Fi.

### Payment failures

A failed payment is the worst moment to lose a customer: they wanted to buy. UPI timeouts, bank OTP issues and card declines happen to every store.

**Fix:**

- make UPI prominent and fast
- after a failure, return the customer to the payment step with the cart intact
- offer an alternative method immediately
- monitor failure rates by payment method daily — a spike usually means a gateway or bank issue, not customers

### Doubts about delivery and returns

Shoppers hesitate when they don't know when the order will arrive or what happens if it's wrong.

**Fix:** show an estimated delivery date based on PIN code, and a one-line returns summary near the payment button with a link to the full policy.

### Trust

A store that looks unfinished, or has no contact details, makes people hesitate before paying.

**Fix:** show contact details, secure payment logos you genuinely use, and reviews. Keep checkout visually consistent with the rest of the store so it doesn't feel like a different site.

## Recover the carts you still lose

Some customers will always leave — distracted, comparing prices, waiting for payday. With their consent:

- send a reminder by email or WhatsApp within a few hours
- link directly back to their cart, not the home page
- a second reminder after a day can help; more than that tends to annoy
- reserve discounts for later reminders, or customers learn to abandon carts on purpose

## Test changes, don't guess

Change one thing at a time and compare completion rates over a few weeks. What works for one store's customers doesn't always work for another's. A change that seems obvious — adding a discount code field, for example — can actually send people off to search for codes.

## A checkout review checklist

- Delivery charges visible before checkout
- Guest checkout available
- PIN code auto-fills city and state
- Mobile keyboard matches each field
- Checkout loads quickly on a mobile network
- UPI prominent; failed payments return to the payment step
- Delivery date and returns summary near the pay button
- Cart recovery messages set up, with consent

Checkout improvements are rarely glamorous. They are, however, some of the highest-return changes an online store can make — every fix applies to every visitor who already wanted to buy.
