Most small businesses forecast in someone's head: "December is always busy", "this product picks up before the monsoon". That instinct is valuable, but it doesn't scale to hundreds of products or several branches, and it disappears when that person leaves.

Even simple, data-based forecasts improve purchasing, cash planning and staffing.

## Start with the question

What decision will the forecast support?

- how much to buy next month
- how many staff to roster for a festival week
- whether cash will be tight in a particular quarter
- what targets to set for branches

The decision decides the level of detail — by product, category, branch, week or month.

## Simple methods that work

### Moving average

Average sales over the last few weeks or months. Smooths out noise. Good for steady products.

### Same period last year

Use last year's sales for the same period, adjusted for growth. Captures seasonality simply.

### Seasonal index

Calculate how each month compares with the yearly average — for example, December at 140% of an average month — and apply it to this year's baseline.

### Weighted approaches

Give recent periods more weight than older ones, so forecasts react to change without overreacting to a single unusual week.

## Where AI helps

With more products, more history and more factors — promotions, holidays, weather, price changes — machine-learning models can find patterns simple methods miss. They're most useful when:

- you have many items to forecast
- several factors clearly influence sales
- historical data is clean and reasonably long

They still need human review. A model doesn't know that a competitor opened next door last week.

## Clean data first

Forecasts are only as good as the history they're built on:

- exclude one-off bulk orders, or flag them
- account for stockouts — zero sales when you had no stock isn't zero demand
- note promotions and unusual events
- use consistent product codes over time

## Use forecasts for decisions

| Use | How |
|---|---|
| Purchasing | Set reorder levels and order quantities from expected demand |
| Staffing | Roster for expected busy periods |
| Cash flow | Plan purchases and payments around expected receipts |
| Targets | Set achievable, data-based targets |

## Measure forecast accuracy

Compare forecast with actual each period. Track the error by product or category, and improve the method where error is largest. Expect higher error for new and slow-moving products.

## Keep people involved

The best forecasts combine data with local knowledge. Let managers adjust forecasts with a reason — an upcoming event, a new competitor, a supplier issue — and record the adjustment. Over time you'll see whether adjustments improve accuracy.

## Start simple

1. Pick one decision — usually monthly purchasing.
2. Use same-period-last-year with a simple growth adjustment.
3. Compare forecast with actual for three months.
4. Refine, and only then consider more advanced methods.

A forecast doesn't need to be perfect to be valuable. It needs to be better than guessing — and for most businesses, that bar is easy to clear.
