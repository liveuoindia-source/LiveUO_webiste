A customer comes back with a strip of tablets bought yesterday. They want a refund. What happens next involves a safety question, a commercial question and a tax question, and most pharmacies answer all three by improvisation at the counter.

A written policy, applied consistently, is better for everyone — including the customer, who gets a predictable answer rather than one that depends on who is serving them.

## The safety principle that drives everything

Once a medicine leaves your premises, you cannot verify how it was stored. It may have sat in a car in June. It may have been in a bathroom cabinet. It may not be the pack you supplied at all.

That means the default is: **returned medicine does not go back on the shelf.** Accepting the return and refunding the customer is a commercial decision you may well make. Returning the stock to saleable inventory is a safety decision you should not.

This single principle resolves most of the difficulty. The question is never really "can I resell this" — it is "will I refund this", and the stock outcome is quarantine or write-off either way.

## A policy that works

### Take back without argument

**Dispensed in error.** Wrong item, wrong strength, wrong quantity. Immediate refund or exchange, no conditions. Record the incident and review it — a dispensing error is a safety event and the review matters more than the refund.

**Damaged or defective.** Broken seal, damaged packaging, a product that is visibly wrong. Refund and pursue the supplier claim.

**Recalled batch.** Refund or replace, unconditionally, as part of the [recall response](/resources/blog/drug-recall-traceability-pharmacy/).

**Short-dated at sale.** If a customer was given stock expiring sooner than reasonable for the course, that is your process failure.

### Consider case by case

**Prescription changed by the doctor.** Common and genuine. Many pharmacies refund within a short window on unopened packs as a goodwill matter. Reasonable, and the stock is still written off.

**Bought in error by the customer.** Sealed, unopened, within a day or two, OTC rather than prescription — usually a goodwill refund.

### Decline, with an explanation

**Opened or part-used packs**, absent a defect or an error on your part.

**Refrigerated items** that have been out of your control, on safety grounds.

**Anything where a genuine safety concern exists** about the returned product.

State the reason. "We can't resell medicines once they've left the shop, so I can't take this back" is understood by most people; a flat refusal is not.

## What happens to the stock

Four possible destinations, and the returned item goes to one of them — never to the shelf:

| Situation | Stock outcome |
|---|---|
| Dispensed in error, sealed, verifiably yours | Quarantine; disposal per policy |
| Damaged or defective | Quarantine; supplier claim |
| Recalled batch | Quarantine per recall instructions |
| Goodwill return | Write-off |

**Quarantine physically.** A labelled, separate location. Returned stock left near saleable stock will eventually be sold, which is the outcome the whole policy exists to prevent.

## The tax and ledger treatment

A customer return is not a negative sale entered casually. It needs:

- A **credit note referencing the original invoice**
- The **original batch** identified
- The **tax reduced** at the rate originally charged
- The **stock outcome** recorded — quarantine or write-off, with value

Two things depend on getting this right. Your [GST return](/resources/blog/pharmacy-gstr1-gstr3b-filing-guide/) needs credit notes linked to originals, and your margin reporting needs the write-off recognised rather than the sale simply disappearing.

> The common shortcut — cancelling the original bill instead of raising a credit note — creates a gap in the invoice sequence and removes the record that a sale happened at all. Reverse, never delete.

## Making it work at the counter

**Require the bill.** Not to be difficult — to identify the batch. A returns process without the original transaction cannot record which batch came back, which is exactly what recall traceability depends on. With customer records, you can retrieve the bill yourself if they have lost it.

**Set an authority level.** Counter staff can process returns up to a value; above that, a manager. Logged either way.

**Record a reason on every return.** This is where the value is. A reason code — dispensing error, prescription changed, damaged, customer error, recall — turns returns from a nuisance into a diagnostic.

**Review returns monthly** by reason and by staff member. A cluster of dispensing errors around one person or one shift is a training signal you want to see early, and it is the single most useful thing the returns data produces.

**Display the policy.** A short printed note at the counter. It converts an argument into a reference.

## What to measure

- **Return rate** as a percentage of sales, trended
- **Returns by reason**, and how that mix moves
- **Dispensing errors** specifically — the safety number, which should be near zero and reviewed individually
- **Write-off value from returns**, alongside expiry write-offs in [margin reporting](/resources/blog/pharmacy-margin-analysis-reports/)
- **Returns by staff member**, for training rather than blame

Dispensing errors are the number that deserves individual attention rather than trend analysis. Each one is an event to understand.
