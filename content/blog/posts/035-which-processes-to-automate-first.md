Every business has more processes that could be automated than it has time or budget to automate. Picking the wrong first one is the most common way automation projects lose momentum: the team spends months on something complicated, the result is underwhelming, and the next idea never gets approved.

A good first automation is boring, frequent and obviously useful. Here is how to find it.

## List the candidates

Start by asking each team one question: *what do you do every day that feels like copying?* You will get answers like:

- typing supplier invoices into the accounts system
- sending payment reminders one by one
- copying website enquiries into a spreadsheet and assigning them
- compiling the same weekly sales report from three sources
- confirming appointments by phone
- updating stock levels on the website after each sale

Write each down with a rough estimate of how often it happens and how long it takes.

## Score each process on five factors

Give each candidate a score from 1 to 5 on these factors:

| Factor | Score high when… |
|---|---|
| **Volume** | It happens many times a day or week |
| **Rules** | The steps are clear and rarely change |
| **Data** | The inputs are already digital, not handwritten |
| **Pain** | It costs measurable time, money or errors today |
| **Risk** | A rare mistake is easy to spot and cheap to fix |

Add the scores. The top two or three are your shortlist.

### Why each factor matters

**Volume** decides the payoff. Automating something done twice a month rarely repays the effort; something done 200 times a week usually does.

**Rules** decide the difficulty. "If the invoice total matches the purchase order, post it; otherwise flag it" is easy to automate. "Decide whether this customer deserves a discount" is not — not yet, anyway.

**Data** decides the starting cost. Digital invoices, web forms and system exports are easy inputs. Paper and free-form phone calls need an extra step, such as OCR or transcription, which adds work and error.

**Pain** decides whether anyone will notice. Pick something people complain about. When it disappears, they become your advocates for the next project.

**Risk** decides how fast you can go live. A wrong appointment reminder is a minor embarrassment. A wrong payment to a supplier is not. Start where mistakes are cheap.

## Typical strong first candidates

These score well in most businesses:

- **Invoice data capture.** Reading supplier invoices and pre-filling the purchase entry for a person to approve.
- **Lead capture and routing.** Website and ad enquiries logged automatically and assigned to the right salesperson with a reminder.
- **Payment reminders.** Scheduled WhatsApp or email reminders before and after due dates.
- **Scheduled reports.** The weekly report generated and emailed automatically from live data.
- **Appointment confirmations.** Booking confirmations and reminders without a person dialling.

Notice that several of these keep a person in the loop. That is deliberate: automation that prepares the work and lets someone approve it is safer to launch and easier to trust.

## Candidates to postpone

Leave these for later:

- processes that change every month
- anything where the rules live in one person's head and haven't been written down
- decisions with legal, financial or medical consequences
- processes that touch many systems you don't control

They aren't impossible. They're just poor first projects.

## Measure before and after

Before you automate, measure the current state for two weeks: how many times it happens, how long each takes, how many errors occur. After launch, measure the same things.

This gives you an honest result — "invoice entry went from four hours a day to forty minutes of review" — which is the strongest argument for the next project.

## Fix the process before you automate it

If the scoring reveals that nobody agrees on how a process works, stop and agree on it first. Write down the steps, the exceptions, and who decides what. Automating an undefined process produces software that encodes the confusion.

## Start small, then expand

Pick one process. Automate the main path, keep a person reviewing exceptions, and run it for a month. Once it's trusted, widen it — more document types, fewer manual checks, or the next process on the list.

The businesses that get the most from automation rarely start with the biggest idea. They start with the most obvious one, prove it, and build from there.
