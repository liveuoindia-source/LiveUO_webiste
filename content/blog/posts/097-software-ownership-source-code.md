You've paid for custom software. It runs your business. Then the relationship with the developer ends — and you discover you don't have the source code, the hosting account is in their name, and nobody else can maintain the system.

This happens more often than it should, and it's entirely preventable with a few clear agreements before the project starts.

## Ownership of code and deliverables

Agree in writing:

- **who owns** the source code and other deliverables written for you
- **when ownership transfers** — often on full payment
- **what you may do** with it — modify, have others maintain it, use it in other businesses

Many businesses assume that paying for development means owning the result. Without a written agreement, that assumption may not hold.

## Pre-existing components

Most developers reuse their own frameworks, modules and tools across clients. That's sensible — it saves you time and money. Usually:

- the developer keeps ownership of these components
- you get a licence to use them as part of your software, without extra fees, for as long as you use it
- the licence continues even if you stop working with the developer

Make sure this licence is written down.

## Open-source software

Almost all modern software includes open-source libraries. They come with licences that have conditions. Ask for:

- a list of open-source components used
- their licences
- confirmation that the licences allow your intended use

## Third-party services

Your software may depend on paid services: hosting, email delivery, SMS, WhatsApp, payment gateways, maps. Agree that:

- accounts are **in your business's name**
- you hold **admin access**
- the developer has the access they need, which you can revoke

## Hosting and domains

Domain names, cloud hosting and app store accounts should be registered to your business, with you as the owner. Losing control of a domain or hosting account can take a business offline.

## Handover checklist

At launch, and at any time on request:

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| Source code in a repository you control | Others can maintain it |
| Build and deployment instructions | Someone else can release updates |
| Database structure and migrations | Data can be understood and moved |
| Credentials for hosting and services | You control the infrastructure |
| Technical documentation | Shortens any handover |
| Open-source component list | Licence compliance |
| Backups and restore procedure | You can recover from failure |

## Source code repository

Ask for the code to be kept in a repository — such as a Git service — where your business has owner or admin access throughout the project, not only at the end. You can then see progress and are never dependent on a handover that may not happen.

## Confidentiality and data

The contract should also cover confidentiality of your business information and protection of any personal data the developer handles during development and support.

## Exit terms

Agree what happens if either side ends the relationship:

- notice period
- handover of code, documentation and credentials
- reasonable help with transition, and its cost

## The bottom line

Clear ownership terms protect both sides. The developer keeps their tools; you keep your software. Settle it before the first line of code is written — it's a much easier conversation then than later.
