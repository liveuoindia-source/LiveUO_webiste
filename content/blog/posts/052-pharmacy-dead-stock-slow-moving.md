Every pharmacy has a shelf it doesn't look at closely. A brand bought because a doctor prescribed it once. A bulk purchase taken for a scheme. Stock from a distributor who insisted. Individually small, together they often add up to a large share of the money tied up in inventory — money that isn't earning, and stock that's ageing towards expiry.

Dead stock is a cash-flow problem disguised as a stock problem.

## Define it with numbers

Agree on definitions so the report means something:

- **Non-moving:** no sales in the last 90 days
- **Slow-moving:** sold, but stock on hand would last more than, say, 120 days at the current sales rate
- **At risk:** non-moving or slow-moving, and expiring within your return window

Adjust the periods to your business. The point is to measure, not to argue about each item.

## Run the report

Your billing software should list, for each item:

- stock quantity and value
- last sale date and last purchase date
- sales in the period
- days of stock at the current sales rate
- batch and expiry for each lot

Sort by value. In most pharmacies a fairly small number of items account for most of the money in dead stock. Start there.

## Why it builds up

Look at the top items and ask how each arrived:

| Cause | Typical example |
|---|---|
| One-off prescription | Brand bought for one patient who never returned |
| Scheme buying | Ten-plus-one offer on an item that sells two a month |
| Doctor change | A local doctor moved, and their prescribing went with them |
| Seasonal leftovers | Winter cough syrups still on the shelf in summer |
| Duplicate brands | Five brands of the same molecule, two of which sell |

Each cause has a different fix, which is why the diagnosis matters.

## Clear what you can

### Return it

Check your distributors' return terms for unexpired, saleable stock, and return what you can while it still has shelf life. Near-expiry stock should go into the expiry-return process before the deadline.

### Transfer it

If you have more than one branch, move slow stock to the branch where it sells.

### Offer it to the right customers

Where substitution is appropriate and permitted, pharmacists can offer an equivalent product in stock. Clinics, nursing homes or other pharmacies may also take specific items.

### Write it off deliberately

Some stock won't move. Recognise the loss, dispose of expired medicines according to the rules, and record it properly. Holding it "just in case" only hides the problem.

## Stop it coming back

### Order one-off items against a request

For items prescribed once, buy the quantity the patient needs, not a full box for the shelf. Keep their minimum stock at zero.

### Judge schemes by sales, not discount

A scheme is worth it only if you'd sell the free quantity before expiry. Compare the scheme quantity with your actual monthly sales first.

### Limit brands per molecule

Keep the brands that sell and stop reordering the rest. Fewer brands mean faster turnover and fewer expiries.

### Set reorder levels from sales

Items reordered on instinct drift towards overstocking. See [setting reorder levels in a pharmacy](/resources/blog/pharmacy-reorder-levels-min-max/) for a simple method.

### Review monthly

Make dead stock a standing item in the monthly review: total value, top ten items, and what was done about last month's list.

## Track the trend

Two numbers tell you whether things are improving:

- **dead stock value** as a share of total stock value
- **write-offs** for expiry each month

Both should fall steadily. If they don't, the buying habits haven't changed yet.

Clearing dead stock once frees cash. Changing how you buy keeps it free.
