Prescription records protect patients, pharmacists and the pharmacy. They show that prescription-only medicines were dispensed against valid prescriptions, support traceability if a problem arises, and are among the first things checked during an inspection.

Yet at a busy counter, recording them is often the step that gets skipped.

## What the rules expect

Indian drug rules classify medicines into schedules with different requirements. Broadly:

- **prescription-only medicines** must be sold only against a valid prescription
- **Schedule H1** drugs carry specific record-keeping requirements for each sale
- **Schedule X** and narcotic and psychotropic drugs have stricter controls, registers and retention

Requirements and retention periods are set by the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules and other applicable laws, and state authorities may add guidance. Check the current rules and keep up with changes. See [Schedule H, H1 and H1X](/resources/blog/schedule-h-h1-h1x-pharmacy-billing-software/) for the detail on what must be tracked.

## What to capture

For sales that need records, typically:

- patient's name, and address where required
- prescriber's name, address and registration number
- date of the prescription
- name of the drug, quantity and batch
- date of sale
- pharmacist who dispensed

## Making it routine at the counter

Recording prescription details by hand in a register is slow and error-prone. Software makes it part of billing:

- items are flagged by schedule in the item master
- when a flagged item is billed, the system prompts for the required details
- frequent prescribers are stored, so their details are selected, not typed
- the bill can't be completed without mandatory fields
- the register is generated from sales data

This takes seconds per sale and produces complete, legible records.

## Retaining the prescription itself

Where rules require the prescription to be retained, or a copy kept, decide how:

- original stored and filed by date
- scanned copy attached to the sale
- prescription stamped or marked as dispensed, where appropriate

## Repeat prescriptions

For chronic patients, record each dispensing against the prescription, and check validity and refill limits. The system's purchase history makes this easier: the pharmacist can see the last dispensing date and quantity.

## Privacy

Prescription records are sensitive health data. Limit access to pharmacists and authorised staff, protect stored copies, and handle data in line with India's data protection law.

## Inspection readiness

Be able to produce, on request:

- registers for scheduled drugs, by period
- prescriptions or copies, where required
- purchase records for the same drugs, by batch

Regular internal checks — a sample of sales each month compared against prescriptions — keep records reliable.

## Common gaps

- missing prescriber registration numbers
- illegible handwritten entries
- sales of scheduled drugs without any prescription record
- records that can't be matched to batches

Good prescription records are a habit, built into the billing flow. Once they are, compliance stops being a scramble before inspections and becomes simply how the pharmacy works.
