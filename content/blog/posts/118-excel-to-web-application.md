Almost every business has one: the spreadsheet that runs something important. Orders, job tracking, inventory, pricing, commissions, project status. It started as a quick fix, grew tab by tab, and now the business depends on it — and on the one person who understands its formulas.

Spreadsheets are brilliant tools. They're also risky foundations for a business process.

## Signs a spreadsheet has outgrown itself

- several people edit it, and versions conflict
- someone overwrote a formula and nobody noticed for weeks
- rules live in people's heads — "never change column F"
- you can't tell who changed what, or when
- it's slow, huge or keeps crashing
- people email copies around
- only one person can fix it
- it holds personal or financial data without proper access control

## What a web application adds

| Spreadsheet | Web application |
|---|---|
| Everyone sees everything | Each role sees what it needs |
| Rules by memory | Rules enforced in forms |
| Formulas can be overwritten | Logic protected in code |
| No history | Full audit trail |
| One file, version conflicts | One database, many users at once |
| Manual reminders | Automatic notifications |
| Office computer | Secure access from anywhere |

## Planning the move

### 1. Understand the spreadsheet

Map every tab, column and formula. Ask users how they actually use it — including workarounds and colour codes. Many business rules are hidden in formatting and habit.

### 2. Decide the workflow

A web app isn't a spreadsheet on the internet. Design the process: who creates records, who approves, what happens next, who gets notified.

### 3. Start with the core

Build the essential workflow first, with the main fields and reports. See [scoping a first release](/resources/blog/mvp-scope-business-software/).

### 4. Migrate the data

Clean the data — duplicates, inconsistent entries, blanks — then import. Run trial imports until counts and totals match.

### 5. Run briefly in parallel

Use both for a short period, compare results, then switch fully and make the spreadsheet read-only.

## Keep the good parts of Excel

People love spreadsheets for flexible analysis. Keep that:

- export filtered data to Excel for ad-hoc analysis
- build the standard reports into the app
- keep the master data in the application, not in exports

## Typical spreadsheets that become apps

- order and job tracking
- inventory across locations
- quotations and pricing
- field service schedules
- commission and incentive calculations
- vendor and contract management

## The payoff

The move replaces fragile formulas and version conflicts with a system that enforces rules, records history and supports many users safely. The person who used to maintain the spreadsheet stops being a single point of failure — and the business gains a process it can scale.
