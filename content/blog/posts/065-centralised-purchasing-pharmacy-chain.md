In a growing pharmacy chain, purchasing often stays exactly as it was with one store: each branch calls its distributors, negotiates its own rates and accepts its own schemes. With three or four branches that's manageable. Beyond that, the chain loses money it doesn't see — paying different prices for the same items, missing volume discounts, and multiplying purchasing effort with every new store.

Central purchasing fixes that, if it's set up carefully.

## What changes

| | Branch purchasing | Central purchasing |
|---|---|---|
| Negotiation | Each branch | One team for all |
| Rates | Vary by branch | Uniform, contracted |
| Schemes | Missed below volume | Captured at chain volume |
| Effort | Grows with each branch | Grows slowly |
| Control | Low | High |

## Two models

### Central negotiation, direct delivery

Head office negotiates rates and schemes with distributors. Branches place orders at those rates, and distributors deliver directly to each branch. Simpler to start, no warehouse needed.

### Central buying, warehouse supply

Head office buys in bulk into a warehouse and supplies branches against indents. More control and redistribution, but needs a warehouse, licence and logistics. See [when to add a central warehouse](/resources/blog/central-warehouse-pharmacy-chain/).

Many chains start with the first model and grow into the second.

## Setting it up

### 1. Build one item master

Central purchasing is impossible if each branch names items differently. Standardise item names, pack sizes, HSN codes and manufacturers across the chain first.

### 2. Consolidate demand

The purchase team needs to see, for every item:

- stock at each branch
- sales at each branch over recent weeks
- minimum and maximum levels per branch
- open orders and stock in transit

The software should combine these into a chain-wide purchase suggestion.

### 3. Negotiate with data

With chain-wide sales data, negotiations become specific: "We buy this quantity a month across seven stores." Agree rates, schemes, credit terms, return policies and delivery commitments in writing.

### 4. Branch indents

Branches request stock through the system, against their levels. The purchase team approves, consolidates and orders.

### 5. Allow controlled exceptions

Some purchases must stay local: urgent prescriptions, rare items, a doctor's specific brand. Allow branches to buy these directly, within limits — a value cap, approved suppliers, and a reason recorded.

## Controls that keep it honest

- **Contracted rates in the system**, so purchase entries outside contract are flagged.
- **Approved supplier list** per item or category.
- **Reports on off-contract purchases** by branch.
- **Separation of duties** — the person ordering isn't the one approving payments.
- **Scheme tracking** — benefits received vs promised.

## Measuring the result

Track, before and after:

- average purchase rate for top items
- scheme benefits captured
- stockouts at branches
- dead stock and expiry write-offs
- purchasing hours spent across the chain

Central purchasing usually pays for itself through rates alone. The bigger long-term benefit is control: knowing what every branch buys, from whom, and at what price.
