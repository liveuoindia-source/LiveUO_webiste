A pharmacy's most valuable customers are its regulars: the diabetic patient who buys every month, the family that comes in for everything, the elderly couple whose prescriptions you know by heart. Losing them to an online pharmacy or a new store down the road is far more expensive than acquiring a new walk-in.

A well-designed loyalty programme gives regulars a concrete reason to stay — and gives you data to look after them better.

## What makes a good pharmacy programme

### Simple to join

Phone number at the counter, nothing more. No forms, no cards to carry. The customer is identified on every bill by their number.

### Easy to understand

"Earn points on every bill, redeem them on your next purchase" is easy. Complex tiers, exclusions and expiry rules confuse customers and staff.

### Visible

Show points earned and balance on every bill, and mention them when the customer is at the counter. A benefit customers forget about doesn't build loyalty.

### Affordable

Calculate the cost before launching. A reward worth a small percentage of the bill, redeemed by a fraction of members, has a predictable cost. A large flat discount for everyone simply lowers your margin.

## Points vs discounts

| | Points | Flat discount |
|---|---|---|
| Encourages return visits | Strongly | Weakly |
| Cost | Only when redeemed | On every bill |
| Easy to copy by competitors | Harder | Easy |
| Data on customer behaviour | Yes | Only if customer is identified |

For most pharmacies, points tied to the customer's phone number work better than blanket discounts.

## Respect the rules

Medicines are not ordinary retail products. Keep the programme within the rules:

- never sell any item above its MRP
- avoid promotions that encourage unnecessary use of medicines
- keep prescription requirements unchanged — loyalty never replaces a valid prescription for scheduled drugs
- many pharmacies focus rewards on the overall bill, or on wellness, OTC and personal-care products

Check any doubts with your state drug control authorities or a professional association.

## Go beyond points

Loyalty is built by service as much as rewards:

- **refill reminders** for chronic medicines, with consent — see [refill reminders and customer retention](/resources/blog/pharmacy-refill-reminders-customer-retention/)
- **home delivery** for regular patients
- **keeping their brands in stock**
- **remembering them** — the software can show purchase history at the counter
- **birthday or festival greetings**, sparingly

## Protect customer data

A loyalty programme collects phone numbers and purchase history — which in a pharmacy can reveal health conditions. Collect consent, use the data only for the purposes you've stated, limit staff access, and never share it for marketing by third parties. India's data protection law applies.

## Measure what matters

- number of active members
- share of sales from members
- repeat purchase rate of members vs non-members
- average monthly spend per member
- points redeemed and their cost
- members lost — those who haven't bought for 60 or 90 days

The last number is the most useful: a regular who stops coming is a signal to call, not just a statistic.

## Launch checklist

1. Decide the reward rate and redemption rules.
2. Calculate the expected cost.
3. Configure it in your billing software.
4. Train staff to ask for the phone number on every bill.
5. Put a simple sign at the counter.
6. Review the numbers after three months.

A loyalty programme won't save a pharmacy with poor service or empty shelves. But for a pharmacy that already serves its regulars well, it turns that relationship into something measurable — and gives patients one more reason to keep coming back.
