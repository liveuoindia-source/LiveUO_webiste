For most Indian sellers, the first online sale happens on a marketplace. The traffic is already there, payments and delivery are handled, and the setup takes days. Sooner or later, though, the same question comes up: should we have our own store?

There's no universal answer, but there is a clear way to think about it.

## What a marketplace gives you

- **Ready demand.** Millions of shoppers searching every day.
- **Trust.** Customers buy from an unknown seller because they trust the platform.
- **Logistics and payments.** Shipping, COD and refunds handled or integrated.
- **Low upfront cost.** No website to build or maintain.

## What it costs you

- **Commission and fees** on every order, plus charges for shipping, payment collection, storage or advertising depending on the programme.
- **Price competition.** Your product appears next to similar ones, often sorted by price.
- **Limited customer relationship.** You usually can't market to marketplace buyers directly, so repeat business is hard to build.
- **Rule changes.** Fees, ranking and policies change, and your business has to adapt.

## What your own store gives you

- **Your customers.** Names, emails and phone numbers (with consent) that you can market to again.
- **Margin on repeat orders.** No commission on orders that come back directly.
- **Brand control.** How the product is presented, priced and bundled.
- **Data.** What people looked at, what they abandoned, what they reorder.

## What it costs you

- **Build and upkeep.** Design, development, hosting, payments, and fixes.
- **Traffic.** Nobody arrives by default. You pay for it with SEO, content, social media and ads.
- **Operations.** Payment gateway, shipping partners, returns and customer support — all yours to manage.

## A simple comparison

| | Marketplace | Own store |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Low | Medium to high |
| Cost per order | Commission and fees | Payment and marketing cost |
| Traffic | Built in | You must generate it |
| Customer ownership | Limited | Full |
| Repeat purchase economics | Weak | Strong |
| Brand control | Limited | Full |

## Questions that decide it

**How often do customers reorder?** Consumables, food, personal care and pet supplies have repeat demand. Owning those customers is valuable. One-off purchases benefit less.

**What are your margins?** If marketplace fees leave little profit, moving even part of your sales direct can change the business. If margins are healthy, the marketplace's reach may be worth the fee.

**Is there a brand?** A product people search for by name can pull traffic to its own store. A generic product mostly can't.

**Can you fund customer acquisition?** An own store needs a marketing budget and patience. Without either, it becomes an expensive brochure.

## Most brands end up doing both

The common pattern is:

1. **Start on marketplaces** to validate demand and learn what sells.
2. **Launch an own store** once there is a product customers recognise and reorder.
3. **Move repeat customers direct** with exclusive products, bundles, subscriptions or better service.
4. **Keep marketplaces for discovery**, treating them as a source of new customers rather than the whole business.

## Making both work together

Running two channels creates one main risk: **selling stock you no longer have.** Inventory needs to sync between your store and marketplaces, ideally from one system. Orders, returns and customer service should flow into one place too, or your team will spend the day switching between dashboards.

Also keep pricing deliberate. Many marketplaces have rules on price parity, and customers notice. Differentiate with bundles, exclusive variants, loyalty benefits or faster service rather than undercutting your own listings.

## The bottom line

A marketplace rents you customers. An own store lets you keep them, at the cost of finding them yourself. Start where demand already is, and build your own channel as soon as you have something people come back for.
