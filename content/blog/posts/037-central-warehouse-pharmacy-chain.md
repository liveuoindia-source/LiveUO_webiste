Most pharmacy chains start the same way. Each branch orders from the same few distributors, keeps its own stock, and the owner keeps an eye on everything by phone. It works with two stores. It strains with four. By six or seven, the owner is spending more time chasing stock than running the business.

A central warehouse is the usual next step. It can lower purchase costs and reduce stockouts — or it can become one more place where stock goes missing. The difference is mostly in how it is run, and in the software behind it.

## Signs that direct buying has stopped working

- **The same item at different rates.** Branches negotiate separately and get different prices and schemes from the same distributor.
- **Stock in the wrong place.** One branch has three months of an item while another is out of it.
- **Purchase work multiplies.** Every new branch adds another set of daily orders, receipts and payments.
- **Missed schemes.** Volume-based discounts and free quantities need quantities no single branch reaches.
- **Expiry spread across stores.** Near-expiry stock in one branch could have sold in another.

If three or more of these are true, it is worth modelling a warehouse.

## What a central warehouse changes

| Area | Direct buying | Central warehouse |
|---|---|---|
| Purchasing | Each branch orders | One purchase team orders for all |
| Pricing | Varies by branch | One negotiated rate |
| Stock | Held in every store | Buffer held centrally, branches lean |
| Replenishment | Branch decides | Branch indents, warehouse supplies |
| Expiry | Managed per store | Visible and redistributed centrally |

The benefits come from **consolidation** — bigger orders, one negotiation, one place for buffer stock. The costs come from **an extra step**: every item now moves twice, and every movement must be recorded.

## Regulatory groundwork

A warehouse supplying medicines to branches is generally a wholesale operation under drug laws. That typically means a separate wholesale licence for the premises, a registered pharmacist or competent person, proper storage conditions, and records of every supply. Requirements vary by state, so confirm them with your state drug control department before committing to premises.

Depending on whether branches are under the same GST registration, movement from the warehouse to a branch may need a delivery challan or a tax invoice. Have your tax adviser confirm the right document for your structure.

## The software controls that make it work

### Branch indents

Branches should raise indents in the system — what they need, in what quantity — based on their sales and minimum levels. Phone calls and WhatsApp lists don't leave a trail and can't be reconciled.

### Batch-level transfers

Every transfer should carry batch numbers, expiry dates and quantities. When a recall comes, you need to know which branch received which batch. When a branch returns near-expiry stock, the warehouse needs to know exactly what is coming back.

### In-transit stock

Between leaving the warehouse and being received at the branch, stock is in neither place. The system should show it as in transit, and the branch should confirm receipt against the transfer — shortfalls flagged immediately, not discovered at the next audit.

### Consolidated purchasing

The purchase team should see total demand across all branches and the warehouse, along with current stock, so orders are placed once for the whole chain.

### Location-wise reports

Owners need stock, value, near-expiry and dead stock by location, plus a consolidated view. Without this, the warehouse becomes a black box.

## Start with part of the range

You don't have to route everything through the warehouse on day one. Many chains start with:

- fast-moving items with regular demand
- high-value items where better rates matter
- items with strong distributor schemes

Branches keep ordering slow, local or urgent items directly. Once the indent and transfer process runs smoothly, more of the range moves to the warehouse.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Running the warehouse on a separate system** from the branches, with manual re-entry between them.
- **Transfers without batches**, which make recalls and expiry returns guesswork.
- **No receipt confirmation**, so losses in transit are never traced.
- **Too much stock centrally.** The warehouse should hold buffer, not everything the chain might ever need.

A central warehouse is a structural change, not just a bigger stockroom. Done with the right controls, it gives a chain the buying power and visibility of a much larger business. Done without them, it adds a second place to lose track of stock.
